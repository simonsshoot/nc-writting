\textbf{Overall performance.} Here, we compare EVOLVE and 4 representative baselines (CoTSafe~\cite{yin2024safeagentbench}, ShieldAgent~\cite{zhang2024asb}, AGrail~\cite{luo2025agrail}, GuardAgent~\cite{xiang2024guardagent}) across 3 benchmarks (RJudge~\cite{yuan2024rjudge}, ASSEBench~\cite{luo2025agentauditor}, AgentHarm~\cite{andriushchenko2025agentharm}) on 4 LLM backbones. Each benchmark covers distinct task scenarios, ranging from multi-domain operational risks to adversarial manipulation of agent behavior, representing emerging risks today that predefined rules cannot solve. As shown in \Fref{fig:main_result}, we can identify:

\ding{182} EVOLVE exhibits strong dominance over other baselines in all 12 test combinations across four evaluation metrics. EVOLVE consistently ranks first, with +9.0\% relative F1 improvement on RJudge over ShieldAgent, +7.4\% on ASSEBench over AGrail, and +41.1\% on AgentHarm over GuardAgent. Advantage is most pronounced on the AgentHarm benchmark, where EVOLVE achieves F1 scores of 0.87, 0.90, 0.90, and 0.91 on four backbone LLMs respectively, significantly outperforming all baselines, leading the runner-up method by as much as 0.40.

\ding{183} Other baselines show highly inconsistent behaviors across benchmarks. ShieldAgent achieves an F1 score of 0.83 on the RJudge benchmark, but catastrophically collapses on AgentHarm benchmark, with an F1 score of only 0.18, a drop of over 65\%. Similarly, GuardAgent, AGrail, and CoTSafe all exhibit F1 fluctuations of 20\% to 40\% across benchmarks, with AGrail dropping below 0.5 on AgentHarm. By contrast, EVOLVE maintains F1 scores stably between 0.86 and 0.92 across all test scenarios demonstrating consistent robustness regardless of emerging risks and threat distribution.

The cause for baselines' failures is their reliance on static, pre-defined settings, which cannot adapt when the risk distribution shifts across benchmarks. EVOLVE avoids this pitfall through its dynamic tool generation pipeline: the generated guardrail tools encode scenario-specific defending logic as executable code, allowing the defense to co-evolve with risks. These observations collectively validate our core argument: treating defense mechanisms as dynamically evolvable code artifacts, rather than fixed rules or natural language constraints, is key to bridging the generalization gap in agent safety.

We then conduct ablation studies to demonstrate the efficacy of EVOLVE on RJudge using DeepSeek through controlled experiments (with or without each module), where we repeat each experiment three times and report average results. The results are shown in \Fref{fig:detail_ablation}. 

\ding{182} We first ablate AnalysisAgent, where we use predefined risk categories for risk analysis without introducing new risk categories. After removing AnalysisAgent, EVOLVE shows significant degradation across all four metrics, with precision dropping from 0.90 to 0.54 and F1 decreasing from 0.91 to 0.64. Accuracy and recall also decline substantially, falling by 12.6\% and 14.7\% respectively. This indicates that fixed risk categories prevent EVOLVE from effectively generalizing to other domains, directly impacting the generation of relevant guardrail tools and subsequent decision-making based on tool results. This phenomenon aligns with the open-world recognition challenge~\citep{bendale2015towards}: when the risk category space is closed a priori, novel threats outside predefined distributions are either force-fitted into inappropriate categories or missed entirely, leading to cascading errors in downstream tool generation.

\ding{183} We then ablate FusionAgent, where AnalysisAgent directly passes newly generated tools to ExecutorAgent for execution without retrieval or fusion. Performance decreases moderately but uniformly across all four metrics, with F1 dropping from 0.91 to 0.89 and accuracy from 0.91 to 0.89. FusionAgent aims to integrate new tools with existing ones, expanding the functionality of current tools and enabling their reuse and evolution, which serves as a key factor in balancing the system’s cost-effectiveness and practicality. Systems that retrieve and recombine existing knowledge components exhibit stronger transfer and scalability than those that learn each task independentl~\cite{lake2017building}, as accumulated tools serve as reusable building blocks for unseen scenarios.


\ding{184} We finally ablate AuditorAgent, where we eliminate auditing of tools and aggregation of results for final decision-making, instead relying directly on tool execution outcomes for judgment. After removing AuditorAgent, the four metrics similarly decline, such as recall dropping from 0.91 to 0.85 and and F1 falling from 0.91 to 0.86. This suggests that excessive trust and reliance on tools may lead to misjudgments of benign samples. Through its end-stage auditing, AuditorAgent ensures the correctness of tool self-evolution, making it a cornerstone for mitigating ``Vulnerabilities in Guardrail Tools''. Systems lacking independent verification tend to accept tool-generated outputs uncritically~\cite{parasuraman1997humans}. AuditorAgent's role is analogous to institutional auditing in human organizations~\cite{weick2011managing}, providing a self-correction layer that prevents error propagation during lifelong tool evolution.

\subsection{Analysis}
\label{subsec:analysis}
\textbf{Generalization to Vision-Language Domains.} To further demonstrate the effectiveness and adaptability of EVOLVE, we first conduct experiments by extending it beyond LLM-agent benchmarks to an multi-modal embodied agent scenario. Specifically, we deploy EVOLVE in the AI2THOR simulation environment~\cite{kolve2022ai2thor}, a visually rich household setting where an embodied agent must execute physical action sequences. Specifically, we construct 15 demo data in AI2THOR scenario. This migration requires EVOLVE to handle risks grounded in spatial context and physical consequences rather than purely linguistic cues. EVOLVE demonstrates excellent capabilities, with 100\% accuracy when detecting potential risks.

As shown in \Fref{fig:embodied}, we present three representative examples where EVOLVE provides clear safeguards. Take the first as an illustration: the original task instructs the agent to place a phone inside a microwave, a clear hazard that could cause fire or device damage. EVOLVE accurately identifies this risk and, through the RevisionAgent, revises the plan into a safe alternative that preserves the user's intent (handling the phone) while removing the unsafe action by placing the phone on a table instead. Across all three cases, EVOLVE does not simply reject harmful tasks but generates feasible, benign substitutes aligned with the physical environment constraints, confirming its adaptability to real-world embodied settings. 

The successful migration is enabled by two core properties. First, EVOLVE's dynamic risk discovery operates over the agent's intended action sequence rather than relying on a fixed taxonomy, allowing it to autonomously infer physical risk categories (fire hazard, property damage) absent from the original text-oriented library. This mirrors the human capacity for analogical transfer~\cite{gentner1983structure}: just as humans apply abstract causal schemas learned in one domain to novel physical situations, EVOLVE abstracts risk patterns from prior experience and projects them onto unfamiliar embodied tasks. Second, the ``security policy as code'' paradigm encodes physical commonsense constraints into executable programmatic checks, which are far more reliable than natural language prompts when evaluating spatially grounded actions. This reflects the broader principle that explicit, verifiable representations outperform implicit heuristics in safety-critical systems~\cite{leveson2016engineering}. Together, these properties allow EVOLVE to generalize from text-based to embodied settings without manual rule engineering.

\textbf{Effectiveness of defending attacks.}
In addition to being able to handle unseen risks, a robust framework also needs to maintain stability when facing adversarial attacks. To demonstrate our framework also possesses this characteristic, we randomly selected 50 risk data on RJudge benchmark, testing EVOLVE and baseline method using four representative perturbation strategies: Multilingual~\cite{yuan2024ciper}, Jailbroken~\cite{deng2023jailbreaker}, PAP~\cite{zeng2024pap}, and ReneLLM~\cite{ding2024renellm}, which cover linguistic obfuscation, jailbreak prompting, persuasion-based manipulation, and nested rewriting respectively. For each method, we report accuracy on both the perturbed and the original statistics to measure defense degradation under attack. Results are shown in \Fref{fig:attack_result}.

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{code/attack_result.pdf}
    \caption{Defense accuracy under four perturbed strategies. For each baseline, the left bar (red) represents accuracy on perturbed inputs and the right bar (blue) represents accuracy on original inputs. EVOLVE maintains consistently high accuracy across all attack types.}
    \label{fig:attack_result}
\end{figure}

EVOLVE demonstrates strong attack resilience across all four perturbation types. Under Jailbroken and ReneLLM attacks, EVOLVE achieves perfect or near-perfect accuracy (1.00 and 0.94 respectively), with negligible degradation compared to its original accuracy of 0.96. Under the more challenging Multilingual attack, EVOLVE maintains 0.92 accuracy, showing only a 4\% drop from the unperturbed setting. This robustness stems from EVOLVE's code-based guardrail paradigm: since safety logic is encoded as executable programmatic checks rather than natural language constraints, adversarial prompt manipulations that exploit linguistic ambiguity have limited effect on the detection mechanism. In contrast, some baseline models exhibited vulnerability to perturbations. CoTSafe experienced the most significant performance degradation, with accuracy dropping from 0.88 to 0.64 in the ReneLLM test (a relative decrease of 27\%) and to 0.72 in the Jailbroken test. This vulnerability stems from CoTSafe's reliance on logical reasoning of the input text, making it directly susceptible to linguistic perturbations that disrupt the reasoning chain.

Overall, EVOLVE maintains high performance in the presence of disturbances. By grounding safety decisions in deterministic programmatic logic rather than model-dependent language understanding, the framework remains robust against adversarial inputs designed to exploit the linguistic vulnerabilities of LLM-based defenses.

\textbf{Effectiveness of the Generated Tools.} In this part, we analyze the effectiveness of the evolved tools on the usage rate and reuse rate. Here, we conduct experiments on RJudge using four backbone LLMs.

\ding{182} Tool usage rate. We first report the proportion of cases where EVOLVE decides to generate a defense tool. As shown in \Fref{fig:reuse_result}, over 50\% of tasks on average rely on guardrail tools generated by EVOLVE to achieve effective protection, with the highest usage rate of 86.7\% on the IoT domain for Claude. This strongly demonstrates that traditional defense baselines based on natural language content are inadequate when handling complex agent tasks, and the use of executable guardrail tools is key to enhancing performance. In other words, EVOLVE exhibits exceptional adaptive judgment, accurately identifying which requests require the invocation of security tools and which can be handled through simplified processes. 

\ding{183} Tool reuse rate. In human cultural evolution, accumulated knowledge and tools are not discarded but continuously inherited, refined, and repurposed across generations (a process known as cumulative cultural evolution~\citep{boyd1988culture,henrich2015secret}), and is considered a defining advantage of human adaptability. Therefore, we further verify whether the generated tools can generalize across different task cases. As shown in \Fref{fig:reuse_result}, the reuse rate on average exceeds 50\%, with the highest rate of 85.7\% on App. of GPT-4o. Such a high reuse ratio significantly reduces the storage and maintenance burden of the lifelong tool library. EVOLVE does not blindly create tools for each new request; instead, it leverages its FusionAgent component to efficiently retrieve and attempt to reuse existing tools from the tool library, iterating and refining them on the foundation of previously created tools. This trend is not unique to one backbone model. In the Application domain, EVOLVE achieves a tool reuse rate of 85.7\% when using GPT as the base model, and 69.9\% with Gemini as the base. \textbf{Effectiveness of Evolution Auditing.} Studies have shown that LLM-generated code may contains security vulnerabilities even when explicitly prompted with safety constraints~\citep{pearce2025asleep,khoury2023secure} and mis-evolution needs auditing~\citep{shao2025misevovle,han2025alignment}. Thus, this part aims to discover whether the generated tools contain security issues and the necessity of evolution auditing. To verify this, we employ LLM as judge to evaluate each generated tool against 10 representative CWE (Common Weakness Enumeration) vulnerability categories~\cite{MITRE_CWE_2025}, determining whether the tool introduces exploitable flaws. %以及怎么判断生成的tool里有没有risk 都要简单说清楚

As shown in \Fref{fig:doubt_result}, over 20\% of initially generated tools exhibit security vulnerabilities on average for DeepSeek and GPT-4o, with individual domain-model combinations reaching as high as 50\% (GPT-4o on IoT). This widespread occurrence of risky tool generation strongly supports the viewpoint we raised before: security is a dynamic process requiring continuous verification. Note that, even when safety constraints are prominently incorporated into the tool generation prompts, the tools initially evolved by the agent may still exhibit related security vulnerabilities. We term this phenomenon ``Vulnerabilities in Guardrail Tools (VGT)", detailed in Appendix~\ref{appendix:VGT}. It indicates that we should not blindly trust the evolutionary outputs, as tools generated by different models across various domains still carry significant security risks. Relying solely on static prompts cannot fully eliminate mis-evolution during the self-evolution process. Importantly, the auditing mechanism in AuditorAgent could mitigate this issue, and we observe that the proportion of risky tools across all domains shows a substantial decline. For instance, on DeepSeek, the risk proportion for tools generated by the Agent in the programming domain drops sharply from 44.78\% to 8.20\%; on GPT-4o, the risk proportion in the finance domain is optimized from 15.89\% to a low 1.87\%.

To validate the practical applicability of EVOLVE, we conduct two real-world case studies spanning distinct deployment contexts: a physical robotic arm (6-DoF myCobot 280-Pi manipulator from Elephant Robotics \cite{elephant}) and OpenClaw \cite{openclaw}, a widely used coding agent with over 320k GitHub stars that autonomously executes real-world tasks including file management, code generation, and financial operations. 

\ding{182} Robotic arm scenario. As shown in \Fref{fig:case_study_robo}, we design three unsafe tasks with escalating complexity. In Task 1, the robotic arm is instructed to pick up a knife and swing it through the air, and in Task 2, the arm is commanded to lift a wooden cube and drop it onto a person lying below. In both cases, EVOLVE identifies the risks and blocks execution entirely. Notably, neither task corresponds to a pre-defined risk category in the original text-oriented risk library. The successful detection relies on AnalysisAgent's open-ended risk discovery: by reasoning over the physical semantics of the action sequence, it autonomously inducts new risk types such as ``sharp object threat'' and ``falling object hazard'' without any manual rule engineering, confirming that the dynamic risk analysis mechanism generalizes to physical domains unseen during training. Task 3 presents a more nuanced challenge that exercises deeper components of the framework. The arm is instructed to pick up a knife and move it along a path where a wooden cube sits in the trajectory. AnalysisAgent identifies the collision risk, but rather than simply blocking the task, RevisionAgent is activated to replan the action sequence: it first commands the arm to relocate the obstructing cube to a safe position, then proceeds with the knife movement along the cleared path. This case demonstrates the interplay between AnalysisAgent and RevisionAgent: the former provides fine-grained risk localization (collision at a specific waypoint), and the latter leverages this information together with the agent's action space to synthesize a minimal-edit safe plan that preserves the user's core intent. The guardrail tool encodes the spatial constraint (knife path must be obstacle-free) as an executable check, enabling precise risk localization that a natural language safety prompt alone could not achieve.

\ding{183} OpenClaw scenario. In this case, we test EVOLVE against a digital security threat. The input request asks OpenClaw to transfer a balance of 5 dollars from a ``bicoin'' account to an external address stored in a notepad. Without EVOLVE's protection, OpenClaw proceeds to simulate the transaction, potentially leading to financial loss and credential exposure. After integrating EVOLVE, AnalysisAgent identifies the request as a potential financial fraud risk involving unauthorized fund transfer to an unverified external account, and the framework instructs OpenClaw to refuse execution even within the simulation environment. This case reveals two important properties of the framework. First, the risk identification operates at the semantic level rather than relying on keyword matching: even though ``bicoin'' is a fictitious trading platform, AnalysisAgent captures the underlying transfer-to-unverified-address pattern and correctly flags the risk. Second, the generated guardrail tool encodes domain-specific financial security logic, such as verifying account legitimacy and flagging transfers to unrecognized addresses, as executable code. This tool can be stored in the guardrail tool library and reused for future financial fraud attempts via FusionAgent's hierarchical retrieval, demonstrating the lifelong learning capability of EVOLVE in real-world deployment.

Together, the two case studies confirm that EVOLVE's architecture, particularly the dynamic risk discovery of AnalysisAgent, the guardrail code tool paradigm, and the intent-preserving replanning of RevisionAgent, transfers effectively from simulation benchmark to real-world systems.
