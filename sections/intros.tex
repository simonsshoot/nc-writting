Building safe and reliable intelligent agents represents a core challenge in the pursuit of Artificial General Intelligence (AGI)~\citep{fang2025comprehensivesurveyselfevolvingai}. In recent years, significant progress has been made in intelligent agents driven primarily by large language models (LLMs)~\citep{gao2025evovlesurvey}. These agents are capable of autonomously managing files, invoking tools, performing operations, and interacting with their environments, leading to their widespread application across various aspects of daily life~\citep{yu2025finmem,yang2024llmpsychology,gur2023webagent}. Nevertheless, the continuous expansion of their capabilities results in a broadening attack surface for agent security, which may lead to greater risks. For instance, a medical agent could misuse permissions to leak patient data~\citep{yuan2024rjudge}, while an autonomous driving agent might misinterpret user instructions and execute dangerous maneuvers~\citep{han2025dme,mao2023driving}. Establishing effective safety mechanisms for LLM-based agents has thus become a pressing imperative\citep{shahriar2025survey}.

Humans excel at reflecting, summarizing knowledge and experience from their actions, continuously learning and applying these insights to future endeavors. This capacity for "self-evolution" is key to the ever-improving learning abilities of humans and serves as a crucial pathway toward achieving general intelligent agents. In recent years, agents powered by large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities across diverse tasks, yet they remain essentially static in nature~\citep{fang2025comprehensivesurveyselfevolvingai}. As more agents are deployed in open, interactive environments, evolving knowledge domains and constantly updated tasks pose challenges to this static paradigm. Self-evolutionary agents are defined as systems capable of dynamically and autonomously improving their own states through self-evolution strategies based on trajectories and feedback generated from interactions with their environment, with the goal of maximizing cumulative task utility~\citep{gao2025evovlesurvey}. Their emergence represents a paradigm shift from static to dynamic, spanning personalized applications, adaptive reasoning, and internal parameter updates. Self-evolution is becoming a new cornerstone on the path toward super-intelligent artificial intelligence (SAI).

Agents based on LLMs are profoundly transforming human-computer interaction. Capable of autonomously managing files, invoking tools, executing operations, and interacting with their environments~\citep{shahriar2025survey}, they are now widely applied across various aspects of daily life, significantly enhancing convenience in fields such as finance~\citep{yu2025finmem}, healthcare~\citep{yang2024llmpsychology}, and home automation~\citep{gur2023webagent}. However, as the autonomous capabilities of these agents continue to advance, more severe security challenges have emerged.
The first major challenge is that inappropriate operations by an agent can lead to greater dangers. For instance, a medical agent might abuse its permissions to leak patient information~\citep{yuan2024rjudge}, or an autonomous driving agent could misinterpret users' request and perform a hazardous maneuver~\citep{han2025dme,mao2023driving}. The second challenge is the continuous expansion of the defensive perimeter required for agent security. Although some existing methods have shown promising defensive results, they largely depend on hard-coded rules, fixed domains, and pre-defined tools, making them difficult to generalize to other domains with different security requirements~\citep{hua2024trustagent,xiang2024guardagent,tsai2025contextual}, shown in Figrue~\ref{fig:intro}.a. Consequently, there is an urgent need for a unified protection framework that can demonstrate excellent defensive performance across different tasks, domains, and environments while possessing strong generalization capabilities and portability.

To address this issue, we revisit agent safety from the perspective of self-evolutionary agents, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:intro}.b. Traditional defense frameworks are deeply tied to specific domains due to their static nature. Some tentative generalization strategies include injecting domain-specific background knowledge and manually designating trusted contexts~\citep{sadhu2024athena}. These methods still fundamentally require human intervention, thereby limiting their applicability. Self-evolution offers a new angle to overcome this inherent limitation. Unlike static, predefined strategies, an agent protection framework endowed with self-evolutionary capabilities can actively extract deep security patterns from interaction trajectories through continuous engagement with its environment. In this dynamic process, domain-specific background knowledge and protection strategies are not pre-injected or statically defined but are instead autonomously refined by the agent itself. This self-induction and abstraction, enables the agent to move beyond simple memorization of known threats and develop transferable, generalizable response mechanisms. As a result, it demonstrates robust and adaptive generalization capabilities when encountering novel attacks or operating in previously unseen domains.

For methods based on self-evolution, a significant challenge lies in ensuring that evolution does not go astray~\citep{shao2025misevovle}. Agents face the risk of mis-evolution, and updates to the knowledge library therefore require validation, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:intro}.c. Some works lack careful auditing of newly acquired knowledge, implicitly assuming that ``newer is better''. This strategy, which defaults to treating new knowledge as beneficial and directly incorporating it into updates, is often the root cause of misevolution~\citep{shao2025misevovle,han2025alignment}. Our experiments show that even when safety constraints are incorporated into tool design, the guardrail tools evolved by an agent may still pose risks—a phenomenon we define as \emph{Vulnerabilities in Guardrail Tools (VGT)}. Safety is not a one-time attribute but a dynamic process that requires ongoing verification throughout evolution~\citep{shahriar2025survey}. Therefore, to build a truly trustworthy self-evolutionary security mechanism, it is crucial to establish an independent auditing system capable of evaluating the new knowledge generated during self-evolution process, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:intro}.d.

Tool calling extends the capability boundaries of agents. Currently, tool calling is primarily used to empower LLMs, enabling them to handle more complex real-world tasks, such as retrieving real-time information through search tools~\citep{shen2024llm}, navigating web pages by controlling browsers~\citep{anupam2025browserarena}, and operating physical devices~\citep{ying2025agentsafe}. Self‑evolutionary agent systems go a step further, autonomously discovering and creating new tools to tackle unknown tasks. For instance, Alita~\citep{qiu2025alita} can search open‑source code repositories online and refine its own tool, Voyager~\citep{wang2023voyager} builds skill libraries via trial‑and‑error exploration in open environments.
In the field of agent security, the discovery, creation, and use of tools introduce a new paradigm for defense. Traditional protection mechanisms typically analyze and filter output content, such natural language based discrimination inherently suffers from ambiguity, interpretative vagueness, and vulnerability to carefully crafted prompts~\citep{liu2024autodan}. In contrast, translating abstract security policies into well‑defined, verifiable, and executable code based tools and then simulating their execution in sandboxes~\citep{ruan2023identifying}, makes the protective logic more reliable than natural language judgments and offers better portability, thereby providing a novel protection mechanism.

We propose our DEFEND framework (\underline{D}ynamic Self-\underline{E}volution \underline{f}ramework for \underline{En}hancing Agent’s \underline{D}efense Ability), shown in Figure~\ref{fig:Pipeline} and Algorithm~\ref{alg:defend_process}. DEFEND is the first to combine agent security with agent self-evolution. Starting from the generation and use of guardrail tools, its core consists of four agent components. AnalysisAgent receives user requests and the protected agent's intended actions, performs in-depth risk analysis, and generates targeted Python guardrail tool code based on a lifetime risk knowledge library, achieving generalization of defense. Next, FusionAgent receives these tools and searches the guardrail tool library; if similar tools exist, it fuses the new tool with existing ones, enabling efficient reuse and precise evolution of existing tools. Then, guardrail tools along with protected agent's actions are simulated and executed in ExecutorAgent, with execution results submitting to AuditorAgent. AuditorAgent independently questions the logical completeness and security of tools, only tools that pass the audit will be stored in the tool library. Furthermore, it integrates all experiment and audit results to make a final decision, forming an autonomous closed loop from risk perception and tool evolution to trusted verification.

By being the first to integrate agent security with agent self-evolution, and treating the defense mechanism as an evolvable and optimizable artifact, DEFEND breaks through the limitations of traditional static defense baselines and demonstrates exceptional defensive capabilities. Furthermore, the introduction of a ``meta-security'' audit mechanism effectively prevents the risk of misevolution. Through AuditorAgent's auditing of the reliability and logical integrity of generated tools, DEFEND addresses the issue of VCT, ensuring the trustworthiness of the dynamic defense system. DEFEND itself incorporates risk knowledge library and tool library with lifelong learning capabilities, enabling the continuous integration of new risk categories and verified guardrail tools. DEFEND supports reuse and portability, thereby achieving automated enhancement of security protection. As a pioneering effort toward unification in the field of agent defense, this framework showcases the potential for achieving universal agent protection and may inspire the development of more broadly applicable agent protection systems.
